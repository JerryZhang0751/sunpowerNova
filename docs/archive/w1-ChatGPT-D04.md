Choosing a home battery storage system comes down to **what you want it to do**, **how much energy you use**, and **whether the battery can deliver enough power when you need it**.

## 1. Decide your main goal

Start by ranking your priorities:

- **Store excess solar** and use it at night.
- **Reduce peak-rate electricity costs** with time-of-use or dynamic tariffs.
- **Backup power** during outages.
- **More energy independence** or future EV/heat-pump use.

Most homeowners buy batteries to reduce bills by charging from solar or cheap grid power and discharging during expensive evening periods [2][3]. If you want backup, confirm that the system can actually power your home during an outage—some batteries need extra hardware or only back up selected circuits [2].

## 2. Size the battery correctly

Battery size is measured in **kWh**, which is the amount of energy it can store. A practical sizing method is:

1. Find your annual electricity use from your bill.
2. Divide by 365 to get daily use.
3. Estimate your evening/night use—often around **40–60%** of daily use.
4. Add extra capacity if you want outage reserve, EV charging support, or cheap-rate grid charging.

For many European homes with rooftop solar, typical usable battery capacity is around **5–15 kWh**, depending on household size and usage pattern [1]. In the UK, many homes can often manage with **5–10 kWh**, but larger households may need more [4]. Australian guidance often points to **16–20 kWh** as a common “sweet spot” where solar capacity is sufficient [2].

Example:
If you use **12 kWh/day** and about half is evening/night use, a **6–8 kWh usable battery** may be enough for solar self-consumption. If you also want backup reserve or time-of-use tariff charging, you may choose **10–13 kWh**.

## 3. Compare usable capacity, not just headline capacity

Manufacturers may list **nominal capacity**, but the important number is **usable capacity**. A 10 kWh battery with 90% depth of discharge gives about **9 kWh usable energy** [1]. Depth of discharge is the proportion of the battery you can safely use before recharging, and modern lithium batteries often offer around 90% or more [4].

## 4. Check power output in kW

Capacity tells you **how long** the battery can run things. Power output, measured in **kW**, tells you **how many appliances** it can run at once.

This matters if you want to power high-demand loads such as:

- Kettle
- Oven
- Heat pump
- Air conditioning
- EV charger
- Well pump
- Whole-home backup

Avoid pairing a large battery with a very small inverter, because the battery may store plenty of energy but be unable to deliver it fast enough [2]. For backup, also check surge power, switchover time, and whether solar panels can keep charging the battery when the grid is down [2].

## 5. Choose the right chemistry

For most modern home systems, **LiFePO4 / LFP lithium batteries** are usually the best choice because they offer strong safety, long cycle life, and good efficiency [1][3]. LiFePO4 batteries commonly offer thousands of cycles, while lead-acid batteries have lower usable depth of discharge, lower efficiency, and shorter life [3].

Good specifications to look for:

- **LFP / LiFePO4 chemistry**
- **90%+ round-trip efficiency**
- **6,000+ cycles**, if available
- **10–15 year warranty**
- **70%+ capacity retention at warranty end**

## 6. Make sure it works with your solar/inverter setup

If you already have solar, ask whether the battery will be:

- **AC-coupled**: easier to retrofit to existing solar systems.
- **DC-coupled / hybrid inverter**: often cleaner for new solar-plus-battery installations.

Neither is always better; the right choice depends on your existing inverter, roof setup, connection rules, and budget [2]. Always confirm battery compatibility with your inverter before buying [3].

## 7. Consider backup configuration

If backup is important, ask:

- Does the battery provide backup during a grid outage?
- Is it **whole-home backup** or **essential-circuits backup** only?
- Can solar continue charging the battery during an outage?
- How fast is the switchover?
- Can it start motors or compressors?
- Is there a backup reserve setting?

Backing up only essential circuits—fridge, lights, internet, key outlets—is often cheaper and more practical than whole-home backup [2].

## 8. Look for modular expandability

Your energy use may grow if you add an EV, heat pump, induction cooking, or air conditioning. Modular batteries let you start smaller and add capacity later [1][3][4]. When evaluating expandability, check:

- Maximum number of modules
- Whether your inverter can handle future expansion
- Whether future modules will remain compatible
- Whether expansion affects warranty

## 9. Compare warranties carefully

A battery warranty usually includes:

- Years of coverage, often **10 years**, sometimes 15 [2][4][5].
- Guaranteed remaining capacity, often around **70%** at warranty end [5].
- Throughput or cycle limits, which matter if you plan to cycle the battery heavily or trade energy [2].

If you plan to charge/discharge daily for tariff arbitrage or virtual power plant participation, warranty throughput becomes especially important [2].

## 10. Check installation location and safety

A home battery should be installed by a qualified professional. Good locations are usually dry, shaded, accessible, and well ventilated [3][4]. Avoid excessive heat, direct sun, poor ventilation, or locations that violate local fire and electrical rules.

Check:

- Indoor/outdoor rating, such as IP65 or IP66
- Operating temperature range
- Required clearances
- Fire-safety requirements
- Weight and wall/floor mounting requirements
- Local electrical code compliance

## 11. Compare total installed cost, not battery price alone

The installed cost may include:

- Battery modules
- Hybrid or battery inverter
- Backup gateway or transfer switch
- Electrical work
- Switchboard upgrades
- Monitoring hardware
- Permits or grid approvals

A cheaper battery can become expensive if it needs extra hardware or complex installation. Payback depends on your solar size, tariff, electricity prices, feed-in rate, and how often the battery cycles [2][3].

## 12. Questions to ask installers

Before signing, ask:

1. What is the **usable capacity** in kWh?
2. What is the **continuous and peak power output**?
3. Is it LFP/LiFePO4?
4. Is backup included, or does it cost extra?
5. Which circuits will be backed up?
6. Can solar charge the battery during a blackout?
7. Is it compatible with my existing inverter?
8. Can I expand it later?
9. What does the warranty cover—years, cycles, throughput, capacity retention?
10. What monitoring app is included?
11. What is the full installed cost?
12. Are permits, grid approvals, and safety compliance included?

## Quick recommendation

For most homes, choose a battery that is:

- Correctly sized to your evening/night use
- LFP/LiFePO4 chemistry
- 90%+ usable depth of discharge
- 10+ year warranty
- Expandable
- Matched to an inverter with enough kW output
- Professionally installed
- Compatible with your solar system and tariff plan

If you have solar and normal household usage, start by looking at **5–15 kWh usable capacity**. If you have high usage, an EV, heat pump, or want stronger backup, consider **15–20+ kWh**, provided your solar system and inverter can support it.
