An **all-in-one solar and battery system** for a detached house usually means a home energy setup that combines the **hybrid inverter, battery storage, charge controller/MPPT, monitoring, and backup switching** into one integrated cabinet or wall-mounted unit, with separate solar panels connected to it. These systems simplify installation compared with separate inverter + battery + controller setups, because the main power electronics and storage are pre-integrated [3].

## What you typically need

For a detached house, a complete system usually includes:

1. **Solar panels** — generate DC electricity from sunlight.
2. **All-in-one hybrid inverter/battery unit** — stores solar energy, converts DC to AC, manages charging, and can provide backup during outages [3].
3. **Mounting system** — roof or ground mounting.
4. **Electrical protection** — breakers, isolators, surge protection, and approved wiring.
5. **Monitoring app/display** — tracks solar generation, battery state of charge, and household consumption [1][3].
6. **Grid connection / backup panel** — if you want grid-tied operation or selected circuits powered during outages.

## Typical size for a detached house

A common full-home solar system is often around **6–10 kW of solar panels**, depending on your electricity use, roof space, and location. EnergySage estimates that an average home may need roughly **16–23 solar panels** to offset electricity usage, depending on annual consumption, sunlight, and panel wattage [2].

As a rough guide:

| Home need | Solar size | Battery size |
|---|---:|---:|
| Backup for essentials only | 3–5 kW | 5–10 kWh |
| Good self-consumption + backup | 5–8 kW | 10–15 kWh |
| Larger detached house / heat pump / EV | 8–12+ kW | 15–30+ kWh |

Battery size depends on what you want to run during an outage. Lights, fridge, Wi‑Fi, and basic sockets may only need **5–10 kWh**, while whole-home backup with heating, air conditioning, or EV charging may require much more.

## Benefits of an all-in-one system

- **Simpler installation** — fewer separate components and less wiring than traditional systems [3].
- **Space-saving** — often wall-mounted or cabinet-style, useful for garages, utility rooms, or plant rooms [3].
- **Backup power** — many units can switch automatically to battery power during outages [3].
- **Better monitoring** — integrated display or app for energy production, storage, and usage [3].
- **Expandable** — some systems allow extra battery modules to be added later [3].

## Important design checks

Before choosing a system, calculate your load: list the appliances you want to power, their wattage, and how many hours per day they run; this gives your daily energy requirement in watt-hours or kilowatt-hours [1]. You should also allow a buffer for losses and unexpected usage, because real systems have inefficiencies [1].

You should also check:

- Annual electricity usage from your bills
- Roof direction, shading, and available area
- Whether you want **grid-tied**, **off-grid**, or **hybrid backup** operation
- Whether you need backup for the whole house or only essential circuits
- Local permits, grid-connection rules, and installer certification requirements
- Future loads such as EV charger, heat pump, hot tub, or air conditioning
