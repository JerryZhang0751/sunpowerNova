When buying **solar panels and a battery**, focus less on the “biggest” system and more on whether it fits your roof, usage pattern, budget, and reliability needs.

## 1. Start with your electricity usage

- Check your last 12 months of bills and note your average daily/monthly kWh use.
- Think about future loads: EV, heat pump, air conditioning, pool pump, home office, etc.
- A useful design approach is to size solar with a buffer, because real-world losses come from inverter conversion, wiring, dust, heat, weather, seasonal changes, and panel degradation [1].

## 2. Check whether your roof is suitable

Look at:

- **Orientation and tilt** — in the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing roofs are generally best; in Australia/Southern Hemisphere, north-facing roofs usually get the most sun [1][3].
- **Shading** — trees, chimneys, neighbouring buildings, and roof vents can significantly reduce output [3].
- **Roof condition** — repair or replace an old roof before installing solar, because removing and reinstalling panels later can be expensive [3].
- **Available space** — a typical 6.6 kW system may need around 30 m² of usable roof area [3].

## 3. Solar panel specs that matter

When comparing panels, look at:

- **Efficiency**: Higher efficiency gives more power per square metre. Good modern panels are often around 21–23% efficient [3].
- **Degradation rate**: Panels slowly lose output over time; cheaper panels may degrade around 0.5% per year, while some premium panels may be closer to 0.25% per year [3].
- **Temperature coefficient / Pmax**: Panels lose output when hot, so a lower temperature coefficient is better, especially in hot climates [3].
- **Warranty**: Look for at least a 25-year product/performance warranty, but also check whether labour and replacement costs are covered [2][3].
- **Certifications**: Check for recognised certifications such as IEC, UL, TÜV, or your country’s approved-products list [2].

## 4. Don’t buy on price alone

Very cheap solar can mean weaker components, poor installation, or a company that may not be around to honour warranties. Compare:

- Panel brand and model
- Inverter brand
- Mounting system
- Installer reputation
- Warranty terms
- Estimated annual production
- Monitoring app
- After-sales support

A good installation company is a major part of system quality, not just the panels themselves [3].

## 5. Choose the right inverter setup

Ask the installer whether you need:

- **String inverter** — common and cost-effective.
- **Microinverters or optimisers** — useful if your roof has shading, multiple orientations, or complex layouts [3].
- **Hybrid inverter** — needed if you want battery compatibility now or later.

If you plan to add a battery later, make sure the inverter and system design support that.

## 6. Battery: decide why you want one

A battery makes most sense if you:

- Use a lot of power at night
- Have low feed-in tariffs/export payments
- Want backup power during blackouts
- Want to use more of your own solar energy
- Plan to stay in the home long-term

Solar panels alone often have a faster payback than solar-plus-battery, and batteries can take over 10 years to pay back depending on your usage and local tariffs [3].

## 7. Battery specs to compare

When buying a battery, check:

- **Usable capacity**, not just total capacity — e.g. a 10 kWh battery may not provide the full 10 kWh usable.
- **Power output** — how many appliances it can run at once.
- **Backup capability** — not all batteries provide blackout protection, and some only back up selected circuits [3].
- **Chemistry** — lithium iron phosphate/LFP is popular for home batteries due to safety and cycle life.
- **Cycle life and warranty** — look at years, cycles, and retained capacity at end of warranty.
- **Expandability** — can you add more battery modules later?
- **Operating temperature range** — important for garages, outdoor installs, or hot climates.
- **Monitoring and controls** — a good app helps you track charging, discharging, solar generation, and grid use.

## 8. Be clear about blackout backup

If backup power matters, ask these questions:

- Will the battery work during a grid outage?
- Does it back up the whole house or only selected circuits?
- Can it run large loads like air conditioning, oven, kettle, or pumps?
- Can the solar panels recharge the battery during a blackout?
- Is extra hardware required?

A standard grid-connected solar system usually shuts down during a blackout unless it has suitable battery/backup equipment [3].

## 9. Check installer quality

A good installer should provide:

- A site inspection or detailed remote assessment
- Shade analysis
- Estimated monthly and annual production
- Clear panel layout
- Equipment datasheets
- Warranty documents
- Electrical compliance paperwork
- Monitoring setup
- Honest advice on whether a battery is worth it

Avoid high-pressure sales, vague quotes, “today only” discounts, or door-to-door offers.

## 10. Compare quotes properly

Get at least 3 quotes and compare:

| Item | What to check |
|---|---|
| Solar size | kW of panels, not just number of panels |
| Panel model | Efficiency, degradation, warranty |
| Inverter | Brand, warranty, battery compatibility |
| Battery | Usable kWh, output, backup ability |
| Installation | Installer reputation and workmanship warranty |
| Production estimate | Expected kWh per year |
| Payback | Solar-only vs solar + battery |
| After-sales support | Who handles faults and warranty claims |

## Quick buying checklist

Before signing, make sure you know:

- Your annual electricity use
- Your daytime vs nighttime usage
- Roof shading and orientation
- Solar system size in kW
- Estimated yearly generation
- Panel efficiency and degradation
- Panel and inverter warranty
- Battery usable capacity
- Whether the battery provides blackout backup
- Whether the installer is reputable and accredited
- Total installed cost after rebates/incentives
- Expected payback period

**Bottom line:** Buy the best-designed system you can afford from a reputable installer. For panels, prioritise reliable brands, strong warranties, good efficiency, and low degradation. For batteries, prioritise usable capacity, backup capability, warranty, and whether the economics actually suit your usage.
