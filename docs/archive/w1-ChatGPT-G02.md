## Solar + battery storage for self-consumption in the UK

Solar PV plus a home battery is mainly about **using more of your own solar electricity instead of importing from the grid**. In the UK, a solar-only home often uses roughly **30–40%** of its generation directly and exports the rest; adding a battery can raise self-consumption to around **70–80%**, because daytime surplus is stored for evening use [4].

### How it works

During the day, solar panels power your home first. Any surplus then charges the battery. Once the battery is full, remaining surplus is exported to the grid. In the evening or overnight, the battery discharges to reduce grid imports. If you are on a smart time-of-use tariff, the battery may also charge from cheap overnight electricity and discharge during expensive periods.

### Why add a battery?

A battery improves the value of solar because **avoided imports are usually worth more than export payments**. For example, if grid electricity costs around 25–30p/kWh and your export tariff pays 12–15p/kWh, using your own stored solar is usually more valuable than exporting it. However, the best export tariffs can narrow that gap, so the right answer depends on your import and export tariffs.

A typical 4kW solar system may generate around **3,500–4,400 kWh/year**, depending on location, roof orientation and shading [1][4]. Without a battery, much of that may be exported; with a battery, more is used in the home, reducing bills.

### Typical annual benefit

For a typical 4kW system, solar-only annual benefit can be roughly **£600–£800** from a mix of bill savings and export income, depending on tariff and generation [1][4]. Adding a battery can increase the total benefit to around **£900–£1,200/year** in some scenarios by increasing self-consumption and enabling smart tariff optimisation [4].

These are broad estimates. Actual returns depend on:

- System size and roof performance
- Daytime electricity use
- Battery size and efficiency
- Import tariff
- Export tariff
- Whether you have an EV, heat pump or immersion diverter
- Whether the battery can be scheduled or controlled by a smart tariff

### Battery sizing

For many UK homes, common battery sizes are around **5–10 kWh**.

As a rule of thumb:

| Household type | Possible battery size |
|---|---:|
| Low electricity use, solar mainly for daytime loads | 3–5 kWh |
| Typical family home | 5–10 kWh |
| High use, EV, heat pump or all-electric home | 10–15+ kWh |

Oversizing the battery can hurt payback if it rarely fills in winter. Undersizing means more summer export and less evening coverage.

### Smart Export Guarantee — SEG

You can still get paid for surplus electricity exported to the grid through the **Smart Export Guarantee**, which requires larger electricity suppliers to offer an export tariff for eligible renewable generators [2]. SEG pays for exported electricity only, not total generation [4].

To qualify, you generally need:

- Solar PV or another eligible renewable generator
- An MCS or equivalent certified installation
- An export-capable smart meter recording half-hourly exports
- DNO notification or approval, usually G98 or G99
- A qualifying export tariff application [1][3]

E.ON Next, for example, states that SEG applicants need a certified installation, a meter that records half-hourly exports and DNO documentation such as G98/G99 approval or acceptance [3].

### Export tariffs and batteries

Export tariffs vary widely. Solar Energy UK’s SEG league table shows rates ranging from very low single digits to 15p/kWh or more, with some higher rates tied to specific installers, batteries or import tariffs [5]. Some high-paying tariffs require that you also take import electricity from the same supplier or had the system installed by that company [5].

For solar + battery homes, smart tariffs can be especially useful. Batteries allow you to:

- Store solar for evening use
- Export at higher-value periods
- Charge cheaply overnight
- Avoid peak import prices
- Potentially participate in automated battery optimisation

Some tariffs are fixed-rate export tariffs, while others are variable or time-of-use. Variable tariffs usually make most sense if your battery can be scheduled to discharge when export rates are high.

### Self-consumption vs export: what should you prioritise?

Usually:

1. **Use solar directly in the home** — best value if it avoids full-price grid electricity.
2. **Charge the battery** — valuable if it avoids evening imports.
3. **Charge an EV or heat water** — depends on your EV/night tariff and hot-water system.
4. **Export surplus** — worthwhile if you are on a strong SEG tariff.

However, if you have a cheap overnight EV tariff and a good daytime export tariff, it can sometimes be better to **export solar and charge the EV overnight from cheap grid electricity** [4].

### Installation and compliance

For a good UK installation, ensure:

- MCS-certified installer
- DNO approval/notification handled properly
- Compatible hybrid inverter or AC-coupled battery
- Smart meter installed and working
- Battery warranty reviewed carefully
- Clear handover pack with MCS certificate, DNO paperwork and inverter settings
- Export limitation understood if the DNO restricts export capacity

Without MCS certification, you may be unable to claim SEG payments [1][2].

### Is solar + battery worth it in the UK?

For many homes, yes — especially if:

- You use a lot of electricity in the evening
- You have high daytime solar generation
- You can access a good export tariff
- You are on, or can move to, a smart import tariff
- You plan to add an EV or heat pump
- You want resilience against future price rises

It is less attractive if:

- Your electricity use is very low
- Your roof is shaded or poorly oriented
- You are out during the day but have a very small evening load
- The battery is oversized for your usage
- You cannot access good tariffs or smart metering

### Practical recommendation

For a typical UK home, a sensible setup is often:

- **3.5–5 kWp solar PV**
- **5–10 kWh battery**
- **Hybrid inverter**
- **MCS-certified installation**
- **Smart meter**
- **Export tariff paying at least 12–15p/kWh if available**
- **Time-of-use import tariff if the battery supports scheduling**

Before buying, model at least three scenarios: solar only, solar + smaller battery, and solar + larger battery. Compare the total annual benefit from avoided imports plus SEG income, not just the export rate.
