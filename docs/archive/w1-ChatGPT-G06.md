## Residential solar + battery storage in France: quick guide

### 1) Main residential options

**A. Self-consumption with surplus sale — “autoconsommation avec vente du surplus”**
You use solar power in the home first, then sell unused surplus to EDF OA. This is the most common residential model. For household PV systems, France’s official public-service guidance notes that systems are typically **≤9 kWc/kWp** and that surplus can be sold to EDF under this option [3].

**B. Self-consumption with battery — “autoconsommation avec batterie”**
You store excess daytime solar and use it later, usually evening/night. This can make sense because the official surplus purchase price for new residential connection requests after **27 March 2025** was reduced to **€0.04/kWh**, making exported solar relatively low-value compared with avoiding grid electricity purchases [3].

**C. Full export — “vente totale”**
All production is exported to the grid. This is less common for new residential projects focused on reducing bills, but it can still be modelled case by case under the EDF OA framework.

---

### 2) Incentives and tariffs

For self-consumption with surplus sale, the official investment premium is now **€80 per kWp** for eligible systems; for example, a 3 kWp system would receive **€240** [3]. The surplus purchase price is **€0.04/kWh** for connection applications lodged after **27 March 2025** [3].

A lower VAT rate is also relevant: Service-Public states that from **1 October 2025**, VAT on PV installations below **9 kWc** is planned at **5.5%**, subject to detailed conditions defined by ministerial decree [3].

---

### 3) Battery storage: when it makes sense

A battery is most attractive when:

- You are out during the day and consume more in the evening.
- You have an EV, heat pump, electric water heater, or high evening load.
- You want backup capability, although many standard batteries do **not** provide full-home backup unless designed with backup/islanding equipment.
- You prefer using your own solar rather than exporting at a low surplus tariff.

A typical residential setup might be:

| Home profile | PV size | Battery size |
|---|---:|---:|
| Small household | 3 kWp | 3–5 kWh |
| Average electric home | 4–6 kWp | 5–10 kWh |
| EV / heat pump / high use | 6–9 kWp | 10–15 kWh |

Battery economics are not automatic: the battery must save enough grid electricity over its life to justify its cost.

---

### 4) Grid connection and metering

Grid connection is handled by **Enedis** or, in some areas, a local distribution company. EDF OA is the purchase-counterparty for eligible exported electricity. The EDF OA process generally relies on Enedis metering data, with injection measured via a Linky smart meter under the OA framework [1].

Typical steps:

1. Choose an **RGE / QualiPV** installer.
2. Size the PV and battery based on annual consumption and load profile.
3. File any local planning declaration if required.
4. Submit grid connection request to Enedis.
5. Install system.
6. Obtain Consuel electrical conformity certificate if required.
7. Sign EDF OA contract if selling surplus.
8. Activate monitoring and optimise self-consumption.

Service-Public specifically recommends working with an **RGE professional** to determine the suitable system size and whether to choose self-consumption without battery, with battery, or with sale of surplus [3].

---

### 5) Practical recommendation

For most French households today, the best starting point is:

**PV first, sized for self-consumption, then add a battery only if your evening/night load is high enough.**

Because surplus export is only **€0.04/kWh** for newer applications, increasing self-consumption is usually more valuable than exporting electricity [3]. A battery can help, but only if its cost, usable capacity, warranty, and expected cycling match your consumption pattern.
